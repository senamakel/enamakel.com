# The anatomy of engineering wealth

*Ten years of notes on money, and the part nobody puts in the newsletter.*

I wrote down a set of wealth principles years ago. Reading them now, most of
them are the kind of advice you get from someone who has not thought about it
very hard. Save more. Buy assets. Compound. All true, all useless, because none
of it explains where the assets came from or why anyone wanted them.

Here is what I think now. Wealth is not found. It is engineered, and the
engineering has three parts: supply and demand, which is just economics;
cartels, which is the historical record of people doing this on purpose; and
game theory, which explains why cartels hold together and why they fall apart.

Wealth moves when you create demand for something you already control. Some
things arrive with demand attached. Oil, Bitcoin. You do not have to convince
anyone. Other things, equity in a company nobody has heard of, have no demand
at all, and your actual job is manufacturing it. That is most of what I do for
a living, and it took me an embarrassingly long time to notice.

Before any of that, though: what is money.

## What money actually is

Money is a medium of exchange, a store of value, and a unit of account.
Currency is the thing you use to communicate how much of it you have, which is
not the same. Money shows up in a lot of forms:

Financial assets, meaning cash, stocks, bonds, funds, Bitcoin. Real assets,
meaning property, land, metals. Personal property, meaning cars, art, whatever
you collect. And ownership of businesses, mostly equity.

The dimension people skip is liquidity: how fast you can turn one form into
another without eating a loss on the way. High liquidity means you can sell
near market price today. Low liquidity means it takes time, effort, or a
haircut. A lot of people who look wealthy are wealthy in the second kind and
cannot do anything about it this quarter.

Keep that distinction in mind, because most of what follows is about people who
controlled an illiquid thing and then engineered the market that made it liquid
at a price they chose.

## The historical record

Cartels have been doing this openly for a century. Legal ones, corporate ones,
decentralized ones, criminal ones. The pattern holds across all of them: get
control of something scarce and wanted, restrict what reaches the market, build
a story that justifies the price, and make life hard for anyone trying to
compete.

What that control buys is not just margin. It funds national budgets, corporate
empires, and in one case an entire parallel state.

### OPEC

The thing I find genuinely fascinating about OPEC is that it is a cartel
operating at the level of countries. If a group of companies coordinated supply
the way OPEC members do, it would be prosecuted. Because it happens between
governments, it is diplomacy.

Saudi Arabia and the other members accumulated an enormous amount of wealth
quickly, and the countries on the receiving end tried to route around it. The
main answer was new supply, most visibly American shale. OPEC's response was to
let prices fall, flooding the market with barrels until a large part of the
shale industry could not cover its costs. Forbes covered the strategy in detail
in [June 2024](https://www.forbes.com/sites/rrapier/2024/06/09/opecs-strategy-to-outlast-the-shale-boom/).

That is the whole move. Not a price war for its own sake, but a willingness to
lose money for longer than your competitor can. Today those countries are
funding infrastructure, defense, and national development programs on decades
of coordinated supply control.

### De Beers

For most of the twentieth century De Beers held somewhere around 80 to 90
percent of the world diamond trade. Mines, exclusive contracts, and near total
control of distribution.

OPEC coordinated sovereign states. De Beers did it as a single company, with
the same logic: hold the supply, control who gets access, and keep the scarcity
believable. Diamonds are not rare. They were made to feel rare.

The famous piece of this is "A diamond is forever," written in 1947. Calling it
advertising undersells it. It attached a commodity to marriage, and once a
stone is what you are supposed to give when you propose, demand stops being
elastic. Then you hold back supply and let the price sit where you want it.

When diamonds turned up outside their control, in Russia or Australia, De Beers
bought the producer, absorbed it into their distribution, or dumped stones to
crush the price until the competitor gave up. Structurally identical to what
OPEC did to shale, forty years earlier and with better copywriting.

They started losing that grip in the 1990s and had clearly lost it by the early
2000s, to antitrust pressure and to producers who no longer needed them to
reach a buyer.

### Bitcoin

Bitcoin gets described as the decentralized alternative to all of this, and the
scarcity is real in a way the diamond scarcity never was. Satoshi wrote a 21
million coin cap into the code, deliberately, as an answer to central banks
being able to print.

But scarcity was never the whole story with De Beers either. They manufactured
theirs through inventory and marketing. OPEC manufactures theirs through
quotas. Bitcoin manufactured its through code and then sold it through
ideology, which is cheaper and travels further.

And the ownership is concentrated. The commonly cited figures are that roughly
2 percent of addresses hold more than 95 percent of supply, that the top
thousand addresses hold over 40 percent, and that Satoshi's own holdings are
somewhere above a million BTC, untouched since 2010. Treat the address-based
numbers with some suspicion, since exchange wallets hold coins for millions of
people and distort the picture. The direction is right even if the precision is
not.

Small group, scarce asset, control of the narrative and of access. The
difference from De Beers is that the holders are pseudonymous wallets instead
of a company with a London address, which changes who you can sue and not much
else.

### The Medellin Cartel

Including this one bothers me, and I think that is the reason to include it.

Escobar's operation in the 1980s ran up to 90 percent of the global cocaine
trade. It owned the coca farms, the processing, the smuggling routes, and the
distribution into the United States and Europe. Vertical integration that any
operator would recognize, applied to a product that killed people and enforced
with murder.

He also built housing and football pitches for the poor, which bought him
loyalty and made him harder to remove. Terror on one side, patronage on the
other. It ended through state pressure, war with rival cartels, and his death,
but the organizational template outlived him and is still in use.

The uncomfortable part is not that a drug cartel behaved like a corporation. It
is how little you have to change in the description to make it read like one.

### What they have in common

Control the supply. Make the arrangement look legitimate. Build the demand.
Push out anyone competing. Keep the concentration intact.

Legality varies enormously across these four. The mechanics barely move.

| Dimension | Bitcoin | De Beers | OPEC | Medellin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Scarcity model | Algorithmic, 21M cap | Artificial, stockpiles | Production quotas | Limited coca-growing regions |
| Legitimacy | Ideology, anti-fiat | Culture, love and luxury | National sovereignty | Populism and fear |
| Market control | Whales, early adopters | Single channel via the CSO | Member quotas, geopolitics | Owned the whole chain |
| Promotion | Evangelism, memes, HODL | "A diamond is forever" | "Stability and fair price" | Escobar's image, plus intimidation |
| Peak control | 2009 to 2021 | ~90% share, mid 1900s | 70 to 80% of exports | 80 to 90% of the trade, 1980s |
| What broke it | Regulation, diversification, sell-offs | Antitrust, new producers | US shale, internal cheating | DEA, cartel wars, Escobar's death |

Read the rows across and the differences start to look cosmetic. Every one of
these built a story that made their position seem natural, and every one of
them eventually met something they could not absorb or outlast.

## What this does to the price

In an ordinary market, supply slopes up and demand slopes down, and where they
cross you get the price and quantity that clear. Standard first-week material.

A cartel shifts the supply curve left on purpose. Less product available at
every price. The intersection moves up and to the left, so the price rises and
fewer units sell, and the cartel makes considerably more money on considerably
less volume. The second effect is the one that matters more over time: a high
price with restricted supply is also a wall, because a new entrant has to build
capacity into a market the incumbent can flood the moment they show up.

That is the mechanism underneath all four examples. Everything else is
presentation.

## Why cartels are unstable

A cartel is a coordination problem. Everyone does better cooperating than they
would competing, and every individual member does better still by cheating
while the others hold the line. Setting one up is the easy half. Keeping it
together is the actual work.

The prisoner's dilemma is the cleanest version of this. Two suspects, held
separately. Both stay quiet, they each get a year. One talks and the other does
not, the talker walks and the silent one gets five. Both talk, both get three.
Reasoning alone, each of them should talk. Both talk, and they land on an
outcome worse than if they had trusted each other.

Swap the terms and you have OPEC's entire internal history. Cooperating means
holding to your quota. Defecting means quietly pumping over it while everyone
else restrains supply, and pocketing the difference. Great if you are the only
one doing it. If everyone does it the price collapses and the cartel is
finished.

Which is why the durable ones invest so heavily in enforcement and in making
sure the relationship keeps going. A single round rewards betrayal. A game with
no end date rewards being someone the others can rely on, because they will be
there next round to collect.

## Why nobody says this out loud

None of this is secret. It is in Forbes and antitrust filings and undergraduate
economics. It is still close to taboo to state plainly, and I think the reason
is straightforward: describing how wealth is engineered undermines the people
who benefit most from it looking like an accident.

The official story is that wealth comes from merit. Work, innovation, risk,
talent. That story does real work. It keeps people participating and it keeps
institutions trusted, and it is not entirely false.

But most large wealth does not accumulate in a straight line from effort. It
compounds through position, coordination, early access, and control of assets.
Say that OPEC runs oil like a monopoly, that De Beers invented diamond scarcity
to sell an emotion, that early Bitcoin holders locked in asymmetric positions
while preaching decentralization, and you are pointing out that the rules
differ depending on where you are sitting.

The part I keep returning to is how much cartel behavior is thoroughly
respectable once it is renamed. Lobbying is influence over the rules,
purchased. Central banks coordinate rates the way OPEC coordinates barrels.
Patent monopolies restrict supply for outsized margins and we call it
innovation policy. Luxury branding is the De Beers method applied to handbags.

All legal. Some of it admirable. Not obviously less manipulative than what
Escobar was doing, and I say that fully aware of how it sounds, which is
exactly why the comparison stays out of polite conversation. The difference is
who benefits and who wrote the rules, and those tend to be the same people.

## Where that leaves me

Oil, diamonds, crypto, cocaine, memecoins. The same shape underneath all of
them. Control something scarce, build a story people believe, coordinate with
the others holding it, and the returns are wildly asymmetric compared to the
effort.

Scarcity is usually a claim rather than a fact, and the claim is what gets
defended. Belief is what turns the claim into a price, whether the asset is
gold or a dog coin. Neither observation is cynical. They are just how the
machine works.

Memecoins are the compressed version, running the full arc in a weekend. Make
the supply, make the culture, coordinate the hype, exit. Oil wells, diamond
mines, blockchains, Telegram groups. The venue keeps changing and the mechanics
do not.

I have not resolved how I feel about knowing this. Some days it reads as a
manual for building things properly and understanding why demand exists before
you go asking for it. Other days it reads as the reason a lot of people work
extremely hard and end up nowhere, and I have not found a version of it that is
comfortable both ways. Probably there isn't one, and the honest position is to
see the game clearly and then decide, deliberately, which side of it you want
to be standing on.
